How to interpret these findings

The recommendations are tied to the five highest-ranked predictors. The study is cross-sectional and correlational, so the findings do not establish cause and effect. Use them to strengthen protective environments and improve prevention, not to assign blame to a person, family or neighbourhood.

Seven implementation recommendations

01 Schools, youth groups and digital spaces

1

Prioritise the peer environment

Peer-related variables occupy the first and fourth places, and the proportion of drug-using friends rose steadily across all three risk groups. Programmes should therefore work with peer groups and networks rather than only with individuals. Peer-educator models and structured, supervised activities also address the unsupervised evenings that increased across the risk groups in Table 4.4.

2

Intervene before first use

For peer approval and exposure to drug-related content, the large step occurs between the Low and Moderate groups, and Moderate respondents already resemble High respondents. Prevention that waits until frequent use is visible will therefore be late. Messaging should target attitudes, norms, and online exposure among youth who have not yet used, and the model’s Low-risk group should be treated as the main audience for primary prevention, not as a group requiring no attention.

3

Invest in digital literacy and the online environment

Exposure to drug-related content ranked second, ahead of most family and socio-economic variables, which supports the inclusion of digital-behaviour indicators. Schools and tertiary institutions should integrate critical media literacy into guidance and counselling, and the Multi-Sectoral Drug and Substance Abuse Plan 2024 to 2030 should give explicit attention to digital channels, which the Zimbabwean literature identifies as a growing route for supply and normalisation (Government of Zimbabwe, 2024; Mugari, 2024).

02 Families and community services

4

Strengthen family communication, then evaluate

Family communication is informative, and evidence of public trust in families and schools, and of the belief that citizens must lead local prevention, supports community and family-led approaches (Masunungure, Moyo-Nyede and Muruvi, 2025). However, because the direction of the association in this sample was not the expected protective one, programmes in this area should be piloted and evaluated locally and should not assume that more family communication alone lowers risk.

03 Health, local government and policy

5

Use the model for supportive triage, with safeguards

Only trained community workers should use the tool, with informed consent and guardian consent for minors. Keep records anonymised and secure. Predictions must lead to supportive referral, never punishment, police disclosure or labelling. Treat Moderate predictions as uncertain and follow them with human assessment. Pilot and re-validate the tool in each new community.

6

Align prevention with a public-health approach

The findings support the movement of national policy from enforcement towards prevention and harm reduction (Sixpence, 2025). Local government and the Ministry of Health and Child Care can use aggregated, anonymised risk patterns to target scarce resources to settings where peer exposure and digital exposure are greatest, without publishing results that stigmatise particular suburbs.

04 Research and evaluation

7

Improve future data collection

Future surveys should use fixed-choice forms to avoid typographical category errors, back-translate all language versions, and record the direction and wording of items consistently so that variables such as family communication can be interpreted more reliably.

Limitations and responsible use

  • Representativeness: snowball and other non-probability recruitment means the results cannot be generalised statistically to all Zimbabwean youth. The sample also under-represents out-of-school, unemployed, older and some geographic groups.
  • Self-report: fear of legal consequences and social desirability may have led to under-reporting; anonymity and trusted referral reduce but cannot remove this risk (Latkin et al., 2017).
  • Risk categories: risk level is derived from survey answers. Moderate combines people with different histories and is the hardest class to predict; treat these predictions as especially uncertain.
  • Validation: performance was assessed with out-of-fold validation within one sample. There was no independent external sample or confidence interval, and the reported macro F1 exceeded the 0.80 target by only 0.02.
  • Feature importance: the ranking came from a flat three-class forest fitted to the full sample, not the chained model. Scores are close (0.0398–0.0613); ranks below first are not firm. Gini importance can favour variables with many levels and can be unstable when predictors are correlated (Hooker, Mentch and Zhou, 2021; Dube and Verster, 2024).
  • Association is not causation: cross-sectional findings cannot show whether peer use or online content precedes a respondent’s own use, or follows it.
  • Instrument and data quality: Shona and Ndebele versions were not formally back-translated; modal imputation and manual correction of data-entry inconsistencies may also affect interpretation.

The tool is for supportive guidance only. It must not replace professional judgement, become a basis for punishment, or be used to disclose or stigmatise individuals.

Priorities for future research

  1. Validate prospectively on fresh samples from the same and new communities, ideally using probability-based or respondent-driven recruitment, and report confidence intervals for macro F1.
  2. Follow respondents over time to test whether scores predict initiation or escalation and to distinguish cause from association.
  3. Extend data collection to rural communities and out-of-school and unemployed youth.
  4. Test importance direction and stability with held-out permutation importance, partial dependence or SHAP, and repeated samples.
  5. Improve Moderate-class prediction by testing ordinal models, meaningful subgroups, cost-sensitive thresholds and additional predictors such as mental health and school engagement.
  6. Compare the chained model with flat classification, logistic regression and gradient boosting under identical validation.
  7. Evaluate indirect questioning, including randomised response or list experiments, to reduce under-reporting.
  8. Audit fairness across gender, age and province, and assess probability calibration before operational use.
  9. Evaluate peer-education and media-literacy interventions to see whether risk-targeted delivery improves on blanket messaging.
